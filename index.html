1. Using a Bunsen Burner
Air hole · Safety flame · Roaring blue flame · Lighting safely
▼

Many chemical reactions need heat to get them going. In the lab we use a Bunsen burner, which burns methane (natural gas) to give a consistent and adjustable heat source.

Turning the collar opens or closes the air hole. This changes how much oxygen mixes with the gas, which changes the colour and temperature of the flame.

The three types of Bunsen flame — more oxygen in = hotter, bluer flame
FlameAir holeFeaturesUsed for
Yellow 'safety'ClosedEasy to see; coolest; makes lots of soot (not enough oxygen)Leaving the burner lit when not heating
Medium blueHalf openHotter than the safety flameGentle heating
Roaring blueFully openHottest flame; hard to see; makes a roaring noiseVigorous (strong) heating

How to light a Bunsen burner safely:

  1. Put on safety goggles, tie back long hair and stand the burner on a heatproof mat.
  2. Connect the rubber tubing to the gas tap and check the air hole is closed.
  3. Light a splint (or use a lighter) and hold it just above the top of the barrel.
  4. Then turn on the gas — a yellow safety flame appears.
  5. Turn the collar to open the air hole until you get the flame you need.
  6. Return to the safety flame whenever you aren't heating, and turn off the gas at the tap when finished.
Exam tip: the safety flame is used when not heating because it is easy to see. It isn't used for heating because it is cooler and leaves black soot on equipment.

2. Combustion — Burning in Oxygen
Fire triangle · Energy released · New materials · Not reversible
▼

Combustion is another word for burning. It is a chemical reaction in which a substance reacts with oxygen and releases energy. The energy is transferred to the surroundings by heating and light.

Combustion is the process behind candles, wood fires, gas cookers and car engines.

The fire triangle — take away any one side and the fire goes out
Recognise the need for oxygen: a candle placed under an upturned jar soon goes out because it uses up the oxygen in the jar. Fire blankets and CO2 extinguishers work by cutting off the oxygen.

Burning makes new materials — it is NOT reversible

When wood, wax or natural gas burns, it forms new substances (such as carbon dioxide, water and ash). You cannot turn ash and smoke back into wood, so burning is an irreversible chemical change. Compare this with melting wax, which is a reversible physical change — the wax can be cooled to become solid again.

Writing word equations

A word equation shows the reactants (starting substances) on the left and the products (new substances) on the right, joined by an arrow meaning "reacts to form". In combustion, oxygen is always one of the reactants.

candle wax + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water
Signs that a chemical reaction has happened
A new substance is formed (e.g. white powder, a new gas)
Energy is given out as heat and/or light
A colour change, fizzing or smoke
The change is usually difficult or impossible to reverse
3. Products of Combustion
Carbon dioxide & water · Limewater · Copper sulfate · Cobalt chloride paper · Glowing splint
▼

When a fuel containing carbon and hydrogen burns — such as candle wax or natural gas — the carbon combines with oxygen to form carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen combines with oxygen to form water (given off as water vapour).

candle wax + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy)

We can prove which products are made by using chemical tests.

Practical: testing the products of a burning candle

Testing the products of burning a candle (or natural gas)
  1. The vacuum (suction) pump draws the gases from the burning candle up through the funnel and along the apparatus.
  2. The ice cools the gases, so the water vapour condenses into a colourless liquid in the U-tube.
  3. The cobalt chloride paper in the U-tube turns from blue to pink → water is produced. (Alternatively, add the liquid to anhydrous copper sulfate, which turns from white to blue.)
  4. The gases then bubble through the limewater, which turns milky / cloudy → carbon dioxide is produced.
Why use ice? Water leaves the flame as a hot gas (water vapour). It must be cooled and condensed back into a liquid before we can test it.

The chemical tests you need to know

Tests for oxygen, carbon dioxide and water
SubstanceTestPositive result
OxygenPut a glowing splint into the gasThe splint relights

Don't mix them up! A glowing splint is for oxygen. (A lighted splint that goes out with a squeaky pop is the test for hydrogen.) "Anhydrous" means "without water". Cobalt chloride paper must be dry (blue) before the test.

4. Fuels & Burning Hydrocarbons
Fossil fuels · Complete & incomplete combustion · Carbon monoxide
▼

A fuel is a substance that is burned to release energy (mostly as heat). We burn fuels to generate electricity, heat homes, cook and power vehicles.

Fossil fuels are coal, crude oil and natural gas. They are non-renewable — they will run out.

Solid fossil fuelLiquid fossil fuelsGaseous fossil fuel
CoalCrude oil and fuels made from it: petrol, diesel, kerosene (paraffin)Natural gas (mainly methane)
How fossil fuels formed will not be examined — you just need the term and examples.

Hydrocarbons

Most fuels (natural gas, petrol, candle wax) are hydrocarbons — compounds made of hydrogen and carbon only. When they burn with plenty of oxygen (complete combustion):

  • the carbon combines with oxygen → carbon dioxide
  • the hydrogen combines with oxygen → water (hydrogen oxide)
hydrocarbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy)
methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Coal is mostly carbon, so burning it produces mainly carbon dioxide.

Incomplete combustion — not enough oxygen

Complete combustionIncomplete combustion
Oxygen supplyPlentyLimited
FlameBlue, hotYellow, sooty, cooler
ProductsCarbon dioxide + waterCarbon monoxide, soot (carbon) + water
Energy releasedMoreLess
Carbon monoxide is a polluting, poisonous gas. It has no colour and no smell, so you can't tell it's there. It stops your blood carrying oxygen around the body. It can come from faulty gas boilers and car engines — homes should have a carbon monoxide detector.

This is why the Bunsen burner's yellow flame is sooty: with the air hole closed, there isn't enough oxygen for all the fuel to burn completely.

5. Fossil Fuels, Climate Change & Acid Rain
Greenhouse effect · Sulfur dioxide · Acid rain · Reducing the impact
▼

Carbon dioxide and climate change

Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap heat radiated from the Earth, which keeps the planet warm enough for life.

The greenhouse effect — more CO₂ traps more heat
  1. Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide into the atmosphere.
  2. This increases the level of CO2, so more heat is trapped.
  3. The Earth's average temperature rises — global warming.
  4. This causes climate change: melting ice caps, rising sea levels (flooding), more extreme weather and changing rainfall patterns that affect crops.

Sulfur dioxide and acid rain

Fossil fuels (especially coal) often contain sulfur impurities. When the fuel burns, the sulfur burns too, forming sulfur dioxide — a polluting gas.

sulfur + oxygen → sulfur dioxide
How acid rain forms
Effects of acid rainWays to reduce it
Kills trees and damages plantsRemove sulfur from fuels before burning them
Makes lakes and rivers acidic, killing fish and other animalsFit scrubbers in power station chimneys to remove sulfur dioxide from waste gases
Wears away (erodes) limestone buildings and statues; corrodes metalBurn less fossil fuel — use renewable energy
Summary of the polluting gases from burning fuels:
Carbon dioxide — from all carbon-containing fuels → climate change.
Sulfur dioxide — from sulfur impurities in fuels → acid rain.
Carbon monoxide — from incomplete combustion (not enough oxygen) → poisonous.
Soot (particulates) — from incomplete combustion → blackens buildings, harms lungs.

Reducing CO2: burn fewer fossil fuels (use wind, solar, hydroelectric power), save energy, reduce deforestation and plant more trees (trees take in CO2 for photosynthesis).
